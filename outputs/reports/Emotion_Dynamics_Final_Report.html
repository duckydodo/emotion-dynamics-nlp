1. Research Motivation and Problem Definition

Most conventional emotion classification systems operate on isolated, single-turn text representations. In natural interpersonal dialogue, however, human emotional expression is fundamentally situated, dynamic, and interdependent: * An utterance such as “Yeah, right” can express joyful agreement, neutral acknowledgment, or intense disgust/sarcasm depending entirely on preceding turns. * Speakers maintain personal emotional momentum (emotional inertia) while simultaneously reacting to social stimuli introduced by interlocutors (emotional resonance / cross-speaker influence). * Multi-party conversations introduce complex conversational topologies where turn-taking is asynchronous, multiple conversational threads intertwine, and speaker dynamics diverge.

This research investigates the degree to which multi-turn conversational context—modeled sequentially via Recurrent Neural Networks (DialogueRNN) and topologically via Graph Convolutional Networks (DialogueGCN)—statistically improves upon fine-tuned pretrained language models (DistilBERT) for conversational emotion recognition (ERC), and how these models illuminate temporal emotion dynamics.

2. Research Questions

  • RQ1 (Contextual Gain): Does incorporating conversational history and speaker identity significantly improve emotion recognition over an isolated utterance-level transformer baseline?
  • RQ2 (Recurrent vs. Graph Topology): Does explicit graph-relational modeling of speaker interaction (DialogueGCN) outperform sequential recurrent party-state tracking (DialogueRNN)?
  • RQ3 (Temporal Dynamics Modeling): How accurately can conversational dynamics (emotional inertia, transition matrices, and turning points) be identified and traced across multi-party exchanges?
  • RQ4 (Model Disagreement & Context Sensitivity): On which specific dialogue turns and emotion classes does conversational context overturn utterance-only misclassifications?
  • RQ5 (Domain Shift & Explainability): How do token-level and context-level attribution methods perform when explaining emotion predictions on real-world conversations outside the scripted television domain?

3. Dataset Selection Rationale

The project standardizes on the Multimodal EmotionLines Dataset (MELD) (Chen et al., 2018; Poria et al., 2019): * Multi-Party Interaction: Features conversations involving 2 to 9 concurrent speakers (from the television series Friends), reflecting authentic multi-party group dynamics rather than synthetic dyadic scripts. * Emotion Taxonomy: Annotates utterances across the 7 Ekman-derived affective categories: Neutral, Joy, Surprise, Anger, Sadness, Disgust, Fear. * Dialogue Granularity: Entire conversational scenes are preserved in chronological sequence, enabling rigorous evaluation of sequential and graph-based dialogue models. * Textual Modality Focus: While MELD provides audio and video clips, our primary investigation isolates the text modality to establish rigorous NLP baselines before introducing multimodal acoustic fusion.

7. Missing Utterance-ID Gaps and Evaluation Cohort Formulation

A critical methodological finding in this research relates to missing utterance IDs in the raw MELD CSV files:

7.1 Root Cause of Missing Gaps

During MELD’s video extraction from Friends DVDs, certain intermediate utterances were discarded due to visual occlusion or audio overlap. For example, a dialogue might transition directly from Utterance_ID = 0 to Utterance_ID = 2, omitting Utterance_ID = 1.

7.2 The Two Test Cohorts

  • Full Test Set (N=2,610 across 280 dialogues): Contains all utterances regardless of internal gaps. Utterance-level models (DistilBERT) can score every utterance independently.
  • Canonical Cohort A (N=2,328 across contiguous dialogues): Excludes dialogue-initial turns and breaks across missing ID gaps where sequential adjacency cannot be validated.

Findings:

Naive concatenation of preceding tokens yields modest gains in macro F1 (+0.8%) but suffers from attention dispersion over long dialogues and fails to distinguish individual party states when 4+ speakers interact simultaneously.

11. DialogueRNN Architecture, Party Tracking, and Implementation

11.1 Architectural Composition

DialogueRNN (Majumder et al., 2019) decomposes multi-party dialogue into three specialized GRU components: 1. Global GRU (G): Tracks overall conversational context and dialogue flow across all speakers. 2. Party GRU (P): Maintains individual psychological states for each active participant, updating only when that specific speaker takes a turn. 3. Emotion GRU (E): Synthesizes the global context, party state, and listener context into an emotion representation vector.

11.2 Recurrent Information Flow Walkthrough

DialogueRNN processes conversational turns sequentially through a multi-stage recurrent pipeline: * Turn Ingestion (u_t \to \mathbf{x}_t): The current turn’s text is converted into a frozen 768-dimensional DistilBERT [CLS] embedding vector \mathbf{x}_t. * Global Context Update (g_t): The Global GRU aggregates overall conversation flow by concatenating the incoming utterance vector with the speaking participant’s prior party state: g_t = \text{GRU}_G(g_{t-1}, [\mathbf{x}_t \mathbin{\Vert} q_t^{(s_t)}]). * Speaker Party State Update (q_t^{(s)}): The active speaker’s private Party GRU updates its internal psychological state using the new utterance and the updated global context: q_t^{(s_t)} = \text{GRU}_P(q_{t-1}^{(s_t)}, [\mathbf{x}_t \mathbin{\Vert} g_t]). All listening interlocutors retain their existing party states (q_t^{(s)} = q_{t-1}^{(s)} for s \ne s_t). * Interlocutor Context Attention (c_t): An attentive query generated from the speaker’s state attends dynamically across all preceding global states \{g_1, \dots, g_{t-1}\} to extract relevant past dialogue context c_t. * Emotion Representation & Prediction (e_t \to \hat{y}_t): The Emotion GRU synthesizes the speaker’s party state with attentive context (e_t = \text{GRU}_E(e_{t-1}, [q_t^{(s_t)} \mathbin{\Vert} c_t])) and passes e_t through a linear projection layer to produce 7-class softmax probabilities.

11.3 Mathematical Formulation

  • Input Feature: Frozen 768-dimensional DistilBERT [CLS] vector \mathbf{x}_t.
  • Global State Update: g_t = \text{GRU}_G(g_{t-1}, [\mathbf{x}_t \mathbin{\Vert} q_t^{(s_t)}])
  • Party State Update: q_t^{(s)} = \begin{cases} \text{GRU}_P(q_{t-1}^{(s)}, [\mathbf{x}_t \mathbin{\Vert} g_t]), & \text{if } s = s_t \\ q_{t-1}^{(s)}, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
  • Attention Mechanism: Soft attention over preceding global states \{g_1, \dots, g_{t-1}\} computes attentive context c_t.
  • Emotion Representation: e_t = \text{GRU}_E(e_{t-1}, [q_t^{(s_t)} \mathbin{\Vert} c_t]).

11.4 Verified Results (Cohort A, N=2,328)

  • Accuracy: 60.14% (+0.69% over DistilBERT)
  • Macro F1: 43.42% (+0.28% over DistilBERT)
  • Weighted F1: 58.92% (+0.98% over DistilBERT)
  • Parameter Count: 670,855 trainable parameters.

12. DialogueGCN Architecture, Relational Topology, and Edge Logic

12.1 Relational Graph Construction

DialogueGCN (Ghosal et al., 2019) represents each dialogue as an undirected graph \mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R}): * Nodes \mathcal{V}: Utterances u_1, \dots, u_N represented by frozen DistilBERT CLS embeddings. * Relations \mathcal{R}: Two relation types: 1. r = \text{self} (Intra-speaker edge: same speaker connecting past and future turns). 2. r = \text{other} (Inter-speaker edge: conversational exchanges between different interlocutors). * Context Window: Windowed temporal bounds (w=10 past turns, w=10 future turns).

12.2 Graph Convolution and Similarity Attention

The relational edge weight \alpha_{ij} between turn i and turn j is dynamically computed using scaled dot-product similarity: \alpha_{ij} = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{(u_i \mathbf{W}_Q)(u_j \mathbf{W}_K)^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

The 2-layer Graph Convolution updates node representations: h_i^{(l+1)} = \sigma \left( \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i^r} \frac{\alpha_{ij}}{c_{i,r}} \mathbf{W}_r^{(l)} h_j^{(l)} + \mathbf{W}_0^{(l)} h_i^{(l)} \right)

12.3 Verified Results (Cohort A, N=2,328)

  • Accuracy: 60.91% (+1.46% over DistilBERT, +0.77% over DialogueRNN)
  • Macro F1: 43.85% (+0.71% over DistilBERT, +0.43% over DialogueRNN)
  • Weighted F1: 60.09% (+2.15% over DistilBERT, +1.17% over DialogueRNN)
  • Parameter Count: 452,487 trainable parameters.

13. Exploratory Sequence Models: Temporal CNN and Linear-Chain CRF

To evaluate alternative sequential paradigms against recurrent and graph architectures, two classic sequence modeling formulations were implemented and evaluated on pre-extracted frozen DistilBERT CLS features (d=768):

13.1 Causal Temporal CNN (1D Dilated Convolution)

  • Architecture: 3 dilated residual blocks with kernel size k=3 and exponential dilations d \in \{1, 2, 4\}.
  • Causality & Receptive Field: Strict left-padding of (k-1) \times d turns prevents future token leakage, achieving an effective causal receptive field of 15 conversational turns.
  • Parameter Count: 823,303 trainable parameters.
  • Training Benchmark: 34.7s on 6 CPU cores across 25 epochs.
  • Cohort A Test Results (N=2,328):
    • Accuracy: 60.27% (+0.82% over DistilBERT baseline)
    • Macro F1: 42.47% (-0.67% vs. DistilBERT baseline)
    • Weighted F1: 59.52% (+1.58% over DistilBERT baseline)

13.2 Linear-Chain Conditional Random Field (CRF)

  • Architecture: Learns an emission projection (768 \to 7, 5,383 parameters) and a 7 \times 7 emotion transition matrix \mathbf{A}_{j, k} (49 parameters) with start/end transitions. Total parameters: 5,446.
  • Training Benchmark: 11.3s on 6 CPU cores across 25 epochs under Negative Log-Likelihood loss.
  • Cohort A Test Results (N=2,328):
    • Global Viterbi Decoding (Non-Causal): Accuracy 60.01%, Macro F1 42.44%, Weighted F1 59.47%
    • Causal Online Greedy Decoding: Accuracy 60.18%, Macro F1 43.15%, Weighted F1 59.63%
  • Learned Transition Matrix Dynamics:
    • Diagonal elements exhibit positive emotional inertia for high-frequency states (Neutral \to Neutral +1.84, Joy \to Joy +0.92), while penalizing rare self-transitions (Fear \to Fear -0.48, Disgust \to Disgust -0.31).
    • Cross-emotion transitions penalize polarity reversals (Disgust \to Joy -1.12, Anger \to Joy -0.89), confirming that immediate polarity reversals are statistically improbable without an intervening neutral or explanatory turn.

13.3 Architectural Synthesis: Inductive Biases in Conversational NLP

The matched Cohort A comparison illuminates why graph and recurrent models maintain a performance ceiling over temporal convolutions and sequence CRFs: 1. Speaker Agnosticism of Temporal Convolutions: Dilated convolutions apply uniform receptive field kernels across time steps regardless of speaker turns. Turn t-1 by Speaker B affects Speaker A differently than Turn t-2 by Speaker A (interpersonal influence vs. self-inertia). Convolutions lack the dynamic indexing required to separate interlocutor influence from personal emotional inertia. 2. Homogeneous Markov Transitions: A single transition matrix \mathbf{A}_{j, k} assumes transitions are identical whether speakers switch or remain the same. 3. The Superiority of Relational Speaker Inductive Biases: DialogueGCN and DialogueRNN condition state updates directly on speaker identity (relational edges or speaker-specific party GRUs), allowing them to break through the 43.4% Macro F1 barrier.

14. Emotion Dynamics: Inertia, Resonance, and Turning Points

14.1 Quantifying Emotional Inertia

Emotional inertia is defined as the conditional probability that Speaker S expresses emotion E at turn t, given that Speaker S’s previous utterance at turn t - k was also emotion E: \mathcal{I}(E, S) = P(y_t = E \mid y_{t-k} = E, \text{speaker}(t) = \text{speaker}(t-k) = S)

14.2 Quantifying Emotional Resonance

Resonance measures cross-speaker contagion: \mathcal{R}(E_A \to E_B) = P(y_t = E_B \mid y_{t-1} = E_A, \text{speaker}(t) \ne \text{speaker}(t-1))

14.3 Turning Point Detection

A conversational turn t^* is classified as an affective turning point if: 1. Significant emotional shift: y_{t^*} \ne y_{t^* - 1}. 2. Valence polarity reversal: \text{Valence}(y_{t^*}) \times \text{Valence}(y_{t^* - 1}) < 0. 3. Divergence persistence: The new emotion state persists for at least 2 subsequent turns.

15. Next-Emotion Forecasting and Empirical Sequence Baselines

In conversational interaction, next-emotion forecasting evaluates whether past dialogue history alone can predict the upcoming emotion of the next turn before that utterance is spoken or perceived. Unlike emotion recognition (which conditions on the current utterance representation u_t), forecasting evaluates pure emotional inertia, interpersonal contagion, and affective transition dynamics.

15.1 Matched Sequence Evaluation Results

15.2 Empirical Analysis of Affective Forecasting Dynamics

  1. The Inertia Advantage of Speaker Persistence (Macro F1 27.36%): Despite posting lower raw accuracy (41.92%), the Speaker Persistence baseline achieves the highest Macro F1 among all standalone historical sequence baselines. Because human speakers exhibit strong emotional inertia across their own conversational turns (Joy \to Joy, Anger \to Anger), Persistence successfully predicts minority emotion bursts. It yields non-zero recall across multiple emotion classes, preventing the minority-class erasure that cripples transition-probability models.

  2. First-Order Markov Degeneracy: The First-Order Markov chain (P(y_t \mid y_{t-1})) produces performance identical to the trivial Majority baseline (47.94% Accuracy, 9.26% Macro F1). Because Neutral constitutes 47.1% of all turns in MELD, the maximum likelihood transition from every emotion y_{t-1} \in \mathcal{Y} leads to Neutral: \arg\max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y \mid y_{t-1}) = \text{Neutral}, \quad \forall y_{t-1} \in \mathcal{Y} Consequently, the 1st-order Markov model predicts Neutral unconditionally for every turn, achieving an F1 of 0.648 on Neutral and 0.00 on all six non-neutral emotions (\text{Macro F1} = 0.648 / 7 = 9.26\%).

  3. Higher-Order Markov Transitions and State Sparsity: Conditioning on higher-order histories (k=2, 3) successfully breaks the Neutral attractor. In Markov-2, conditioning on 49 unique (y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}) bigram pairs lifts Macro F1 to 20.03%, capturing strong emotional continuities such as Anger \to Anger (F1 = 0.39), Joy \to Joy (F1 = 0.20), and Sadness (F1 = 0.18). However, moving to Markov-3 yields only a marginal Macro F1 gain (20.80%) while overall accuracy declines to 47.65%. This plateau is driven by severe combinatorial state sparsity: while 7^3 = 343 transition states are theoretically possible, only 311 are observed in training, with over 60% of observed states having support N \le 2.

  4. Compounding Error in Recursive Forecasting: When forecasting multiple turns into the future in a closed loop (recursive generation without teacher forcing), prediction divergence reaches 24.14% for Markov-2 and 24.52% for Markov-3. As small forecasting errors accumulate, the sequential trajectory quickly collapses back into the stationary Neutral distribution, degrading Macro F1 to 15.16% and 13.45% respectively.

16. Explainability Methods and Attributions

To avoid deceptive certainty, explanations are decoupled into three empirical layers:

16.1 Text-Level Token Attribution (Integrated Gradients)

Following Sundararajan et al. (2017), path-integrated gradients (M=25 steps) compute the attribution of subword token embeddings toward classification logits: \text{Attribution}_i^c(x) \approx (x_i - x'_i) \times \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left. \frac{\partial F_c}{\partial x_i} \right|_{x' + \frac{m}{M}(x - x')} * Sarcasm Lexical Trap: On the utterance “Oh that’s just fantastic, another flat tire in the middle of a thunderstorm.”, DistilBERT falsely predicts Joy with 89.9\% confidence because "fantastic" receives the highest positive attribution (+0.242). This empirically proves the vulnerability of surface text transformers to sarcastic irony in the absence of vocal prosody or conversational context. * Affective Token Salience: Across true positive classes, attribution concentrates on semantic pivots: "disgusting" (+0.339) for Disgust, "terrified" (+0.159) and "leave" (+0.135) for Fear, and discourse markers "wait" (+0.159) and "serious" (+0.160) for Surprise.

16.2 Conversational Context Perturbation (\Delta P, \Delta \ell)

Evaluating turns under full conversational history \mathcal{H}_{<t} versus isolated utterance conditions (0-context) isolates conversational sensitivity: \Delta P_t(y) = P(y \mid u_t, \mathcal{H}_{<t}) - P(y \mid u_t, \emptyset) * In an emotional escalation dialogue between Ross and Rachel (“I made a mistake, okay? It didn’t mean anything!”), isolated DialogueRNN predicts Anger with 0.77 confidence. Under full context, preceding rejection (“We are done”) introduces conflicting affective history into Ross’s party state, shifting confidence down by \Delta P = -0.314 (0.46) as probability distributes toward distress. * Conversely, DialogueGCN leverages relational speaker edges to reinforce conflict dynamics, boosting Anger confidence by +0.113 (1.00).

16.3 Cross-Model Disagreement Taxonomy (Cohort A, N=2,328)

Matching test predictions across DistilBERT, DialogueRNN, and DialogueGCN reveals high pairwise concordance and illuminates exact context failure modes: * Unanimous Agreement: All 3 models agree on 1,772 out of 2,328 turns (76.12\%). * Pairwise Cohen’s Kappa: * DistilBERT vs. DialogueRNN: \kappa = 0.8412 (Concordance: 88.66\%) * DialogueRNN vs. DialogueGCN: \kappa = 0.7468 (Concordance: 81.44\%) * DistilBERT vs. DialogueGCN: \kappa = 0.7116 (Concordance: 78.91\%) * Taxonomy Distribution: * All Correct: 1,210 turns (51.98\%) * Context Recovery (Net): 197 turns (8.46\%) where DistilBERT failed but context models recovered ground truth (107 unique DialogueGCN wins, 30 DialogueRNN wins, and 60 mutual wins). * Context Dilution: 174 turns (7.47\%) where isolated DistilBERT was correct, but conversational history over-smoothed the prediction into an error. * Consensus Failure: 10 turns (0.43\%) where all 3 models predicted different emotions. * Universal Error: 737 turns (31.66\%) where all 3 models failed, reflecting the ceiling of textual ERC on short backchannels and subtle sarcasm without multimodal audio.

18. Methodological and Domain Limitations

  1. MELD Domain Shift: Scripted situation comedy dialogue features elevated rates of sarcasm, exaggerated vocalizations, and comedic timing that do not mirror clinical or casual interpersonal text.
  2. Annotation Ambiguity: Human inter-annotator agreement on MELD achieves Fleiss’ \kappa \approx 0.43, reflecting genuine affective ambiguity.
  3. Non-Clinical Boundary: Predictions reflect perceived expressed emotion in text, not internal psychological reality.

19. Future Multimodal Research Scope: Tri-Modal Affective Fusion

While the core contributions of this study establish textual and contextual baselines for multi-party ERC, MELD natively provides paired audiovisual recordings for every dialogue turn (.mp4 clips). As demonstrated by our Integrated Gradients error analysis, text-only ERC suffers from fundamental ambiguity in sarcastic irony, backchannel vocalizations (“Oh”, “Yeah”), and non-verbal affective leakage. We establish a concrete architectural blueprint for extending the current pipeline into full Tri-Modal Affective Fusion (Text + Audio + Video):

19.1 Acoustic and Prosodic Feature Engineering

Acoustic signals capture vocal arousal, pitch inflection, and affective intensity that are invisible in text transcriptions: 1. Expert Engineered Prosody (openSMILE eGeMAPS): Extraction of the 88-dimensional extended Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set (eGeMAPS), including fundamental frequency (F_0 pitch trajectory, jitter), energy dynamics (loudness, shimmer), spectral balance (formants F_1-F_3, alpha ratio), and harmonic-to-noise ratio (HNR). 2. Self-Supervised Speech Representations (WavLM / HuBERT): Dense acoustic representations extracted from pre-trained WavLM-Large or HuBERT-Large (1024-dim), pooling frame-level transformer layers to preserve paralinguistic tone, pauses, and emotional tremors. 3. Temporal ASR Word Alignment: Utilizing OpenAI Whisper-Large-v3 forced alignment to timestamp phoneme-to-word boundaries, ensuring microsecond-level synchronization between acoustic pitch contours and subword textual tokens.

19.2 Visual Facial Expression and Gestural Dynamics

Visual information resolves ambiguity through micro-expressions and gaze dynamics: 1. Facial Action Coding System (OpenFace 2.0 FACS): Extraction of 18 continuous Action Unit (AU) intensities (e.g., AU01 Inner Brow Raiser, AU04 Brow Lowerer, AU12 Lip Corner Puller, AU15 Lip Corner Depressor), alongside 3D head pose orientation (pitch, yaw, roll) and eye gaze vectors. 2. Spatiotemporal Visual Embeddings (VideoMAE / CLIP): Extracting frame-level face-crop representations using CLIP-ViT-B/16 and temporal transformer encoders (VideoMAE-v2), capturing dynamic facial gestures and muscle tension across the utterance duration.

19.3 Tri-Modal Relational Fusion Architecture

Rather than simple late-stage heuristic voting, multimodal features are fused at the utterance level before sequential dialogue modeling: \mathbf{u}_t = \text{LayerNorm}\left( \mathbf{W}_f \left[ \mathbf{e}_{\text{text},t} \mathbin{\Vert} \mathbf{a}_{\text{audio},t} \mathbin{\Vert} \mathbf{v}_{\text{video},t} \right] + \mathbf{b}_f \right)

Alternatively, Cross-Modal Multi-Head Attention allows textual tokens to query acoustic and visual feature maps, selectively amplifying vocal stress cues when textual sentiment is ambiguous: \mathbf{h}_{\text{cross}, t} = \text{MultiHeadAttention}\left(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{e}_{\text{text},t}, \, \mathbf{K}=\mathbf{a}_{\text{audio},t}, \, \mathbf{V}=\mathbf{a}_{\text{audio},t}\right)

These fused tri-modal utterance representations \mathbf{u}_t directly replace unimodal DistilBERT features in: * DialogueRNN: Modulating speaker party state GRUs (q_t^{(s_t)} = \text{GRU}(q_{t-1}^{(s_t)}, [\mathbf{u}_t \mathbin{\Vert} c_t])), capturing how a speaker’s vocal tension accumulates over time. * DialogueGCN: Modulating relational edge weights (\alpha_{ij} = \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{W}_e \mathbf{u}_j)), allowing interlocutor graph attention to weight speakers who express elevated vocal or visual agitation.

19.4 Real-World Application: Conversational De-Escalation Index

For deployment in customer resolution, conflict mediation, and mental health support, tri-modal emotion dynamics provide an actionable De-Escalation Index (D_{\text{idx}}): D_{\text{idx}}(t) = \sum_{\tau=t-k}^{t} w_\tau \cdot \left[ \Delta \text{Valence}(\tau) - \lambda \cdot \Delta \text{Arousal}(\tau) \right] Where w_\tau is an exponential decay recency weight, \text{Valence} is derived from text and facial smiles (AU12), and \text{Arousal} is derived from acoustic volume and pitch velocity. A consistently negative D_{\text{idx}} triggers real-time early-warning intervention prompts before conversational breakdown occurs.
